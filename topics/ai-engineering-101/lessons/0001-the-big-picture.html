1 · A language model is a completion machine

A language model encodes statistics about language: given some context, how likely each next piece of text is. Its basic unit is the token, which can be a word, part of a word, or a character. For GPT-4 a token averages about ¾ of a word, so 100 tokens ≈ 75 words. Ch1 · Language models

The models this book is about are autoregressive: they predict the next token, append it, and repeat. That makes them completion machines. Give one a prompt and it continues the text. Many tasks fit this shape:

Question: Is this email likely spam? Here's the email: <email>
Answer: Likely spam Ch. 1's example of turning a completion machine into a spam classifier

One consequence runs through the whole book. Completions are predictions based on probabilities, so they aren't guaranteed to be correct. That probabilistic nature makes these models “both so exciting and frustrating to use.” Ch1 Chapter 2 explains the mechanism (sampling) and Chapters 3–4 explain how to live with it (evaluation).

2 · Why they got so big: self-supervision

Older successes such as the AlexNet image classifier needed supervision: humans labeling every example. Labeling costs money, and that capped how big the datasets could get. Language modeling is self-supervised, because any text already contains its own labels. Each next token is the answer for the tokens before it:

The internet is full of text, so training data could grow enormously, and models grew with it into LLMs. Add images, audio, and other modalities and you get a foundation model: a general-purpose model that you build on instead of training a new model for each task. 3 · AI engineering vs ML engineering

AI engineering is building applications on top of foundation models. Huyen names three big shifts from traditional ML engineering: Ch1 · AI Engineering Versus ML Engineering

  1. Adapt, don't train. Someone else trained the model. Your work is model adaptation.
  2. Bigger, slower, costlier models. That puts more pressure on inference optimization and more need for engineers who can work with GPUs.
  3. Open-ended outputs. A spam filter is either right or wrong. A chatbot answer has endless valid forms, which makes evaluation a much bigger problem.
AI engineering differs from ML engineering in that it's less about model development and more about adapting and evaluating models.Chip Huyen, AI Engineering, Ch. 1

4 · Two ways to adapt a model

All adaptation techniques fall into two families, split by one question: do the model's weights change?

Prompt-basedFinetuning
WeightsUnchanged; you supply instructions and contextUpdated by further training
ExamplesPrompt engineering, RAGFinetuning, incl. parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA
Trade-offEasy to start, needs little data, lets you try many modelsMore complex, needs more data; can improve quality, latency, and cost

Huyen's running example is generating product descriptions for a retailer. You can write detailed instructions with examples (prompt engineering), connect the model to a database of customer reviews (RAG), or further train it on great descriptions (finetuning). Adapting an existing model instead of building one is the difference between “ten examples and one weekend” and “1 million examples and six months.” Ch1

Watch your wordsPasting your journal into ChatGPT so it “sounds like you” is not training or finetuning. Teaching a model through its context is prompt engineering. Huyen calls out this mix-up explicitly. Ch1 · Training vs Pre-Training vs Finetuning vs Post-Training
